How PIDs Work: Who Pays, and a Fix Already Working in Utah

Posted Oct. 29, 2026 · Updated Oct. 29, 2026

A Public Infrastructure District, or PID, borrows money to build roads and pipes for a new development, and the people who later own the land pay it back. This page shows how that works in Utah today, who carries the cost, what federal regulators and the State Auditor said at the Capitol on Oct. 19, what the Inland Port and MIDA told lawmakers on Oct. 21, and a rule three Utah districts already use so families don't inherit the bill.

The short version

  • A PID's bill rides with the house. Utah law does not require a seller to pay it off. It only requires showing the buyer the yearly cost at or before closing (Utah Code 57-1-49).
  • Households already carry about $529 a year from 15 property-tax hikes that took effect in Weber County since 2022, on a median home. A PID bill comes on top of that.
  • The SEC's municipal securities director came to the Capitol on Oct. 19 and said PIDs have "real potential to become a problem if not handled correctly." He called it surprising that Utah lets state development authorities collect sales tax to repay bonds.
  • Nine Weber County taxing bodies' property tax budgets rose 92% from 2018 to 2026 (State Tax Commission approved budgets), while about $12 million to $14 million a year in growth went to project areas instead.
  • The State Auditor counts nearly 250 PIDs and similar districts, $5.3 billion borrowed, and fewer than half meeting state transparency rules.
  • Three Utah districts already require payoff first: Tech Ridge (St. George), Firefly (Eagle Mountain) and Sawmill (Wasatch County). The reform would make that the rule for new PIDs.

What a PID is

A Public Infrastructure District is a small public district that borrows money to build roads, water and sewer for a new development. It starts on raw land. Utah law requires consent from 100% of the surface property owners (easements held by utilities and public bodies don't count), and at that point the developer is usually the only owner (Utah Code 17D-4-201(1)(a)).

The debt is repaid by the property, through a yearly bill tied to the land. The board is appointed at first, often people who represent the property owners; seats can later move to elections (17D-4-202).

Two kinds of PID bills

The idea behind the reform, in one line: help the developer up front, clear each lot's share at the first sale, and every home sells on equal footing after that. The cost doesn't disappear. It's paid once, up front, usually inside the home price, instead of year after year.

How a district starts and grows

1. City or county approves the PID and its map
↓
2. Developer signs as 100% of the owners
↓
3. Appointed board borrows (bonds)
↓
4. Roads, water and sewer are built
↓
5. Homes sell; the yearly bill goes with each home
↓
6. Board adds more land the developer owns. No new city or county vote. Back to step 3.

How it grows: the governing document pre-sets an annexation area. The developer signs for the new land as its only owner, and the board approves it by resolution. No new city or county vote is needed, unless the land is outside the city or county that created it (Utah Code 17D-4-201(3)).

Weber County example: on Jan. 20, 2026, the Promontory Commerce Center PID No. 1 board annexed about 329.5 acres this way (Resolution 2026-01). The same clause is in Pine View No. 2, Three Bridges, BZI Innovation Park and Oquirrh Point.

Who carries the cost today

The assessment is recorded against the land. When the house sells, the bill goes with it to the next owner, and the next, for decades.

  • Of 8 districts we checked, 5 let an owner pay it off early only if they choose to. Utah has many more; we have not checked them all.
  • Utah law does not require a seller to clear it. It only requires showing the buyer the yearly cost of the PID's tax rate at or before closing (H.B. 507, 2026; Utah Code 57-1-49). A buyer who learns that late and backs out can lose their earnest money.
  • An early version of a 2026 bill (H.B. 422) let a buyer who wasn't told back out and keep their earnest money. That was dropped, and the bill did not pass.

Who else feels it

The PID assessment itself stays inside the district line. But when a project is also paired with tax increment (a CRA or TIF), the growth in property tax goes to the project for years instead of to the county, schools and fire district. Those entities still serve the new homes. The gap shows up as rate pressure on everyone else, or as fewer services.

Nordic Village example: the financing plan shows Weber Fire District getting $428,158 a year while it participates, then $856,315 a year once participation ends. Double, in the agency's own model.

Condos and apartments

Condos are sold unit by unit, so the bill passes to each buyer the same way it does with a house. Apartments are never sold to the people living there. The owner pays the bill, and that cost can be built into the rent. A rule for rentals already exists: Sawmill IFD in Wasatch County requires homes to be paid off at final inspection, and apartments about a year after they open, or sooner if sold to an unrelated buyer.

Second homes: owners vote where they live. If homes are sold as second homes, those owners pay these bills but cannot vote for the local officials who set them.

What the $529 is, and what it isn't

  • It is not a PID bill. These increases were adopted by the school district, fire district, county, sewer district, water district and dispatch center, and every household in those districts pays them, inside a PID or not.
  • But growth deals and these hikes are connected. When a project is paired with tax increment (a CRA, TIF, or an Inland Port or MIDA project area), the growth in property tax goes to the project for years. The school district, fire district and county still have to serve the new homes and businesses, without that growth money. When the money isn't coming in, the difference has to be made up somewhere: by higher rates on everyone else, or by fewer services. In the Nordic Village plan, Weber Fire District gets $428,158 a year while it shares its increment and $856,315 once that ends. The records show how much is held back; they don't show how much of any single hike came from it.
  • How much growth money is held back in Weber County, from the Hive's research:
    • Nordic Village CRA (adopted Dec. 2024): Weber County shares 75% of its increment for 15 years; Weber School District, Weber Fire District and Weber Basin Water each share 50% for 15 years. Weber Basin's projected share is about $965,000 over the agreement. Weber Fire's own model: $428,158 a year while it shares, $856,315 once that ends.
    • Chromalox CRA: about $4.13 million in projected property tax for infrastructure, incentives and administration; the county shares for 15 years, the school and fire districts for 10.
    • Inland Port, West Weber (about 8,968 acres): 75% of the growth goes to UIPA for 25 years per parcel once triggered; the trigger date has slid from 2025 to 2027.
    • MIDA, Falcon Hill: about 525 acres touching Roy, Clearfield, Riverdale and Sunset.
    • West Bench RDA: increment collected 2020 through 2025.
    None of the increase notices the Hive reviewed names increment sharing as a reason; they cite pay, staffing, inflation, required upgrades and new buildings. But sharing reduces the growth money these entities would otherwise have to meet those costs, and for most of these projects it has only just begun. Sources: The Quiet Machine; Every Weber County Property Tax Increase Since 2019.
  • It is the starting point. A family that buys a home inside a PID pays this $529 and the PID's own yearly bill on top of it. For example, a 5-mill PID levy like Nordic Village's would add about $1,195 a year on this same median home (0.005 × $239,020.65 taxable value).
  • It leaves things out, so the real total is higher:
    • City tax increases, such as Roy's 2026 increase (about $133 a year on a $439,000 home)
    • Utility fees, such as Central Weber Sewer's 8% service fee that started July 1, 2026
    • Weber County's 0.20% sales tax, added in 2026
    • Any PID assessment or levy

And it's not only property tax

In 2026, Weber County also added a 0.20% sales tax for public safety and transportation, approved by the three commissioners with no public vote. It's expected to bring in about $13.1 million a year: $2 more for every $1,000 of taxable purchases, or about $40 a year for a household spending $20,000. Sales tax does not require a Truth in Taxation notice or hearing.

The median home, 20 years apart

Weber County home values grew 2.9 times from 2005 to 2025, about 5.5% a year (FHFA House Price Index: 110.35 in 2005, 323.73 in 2025). Today's $434,583 median would have been about $148,000 in 2005. If the next 20 years match the last 20, it would be about $1.27 million in 2045. That's a what-if, not a forecast.

What the records don't show: that the held-back money caused the increases. The held-back money is about 4% of these budgets. Most of the budget growth came from new construction, which adds revenue without a hearing, and from the increases themselves. None of the increase notices names increment sharing as a reason. What the SEC and the State Auditor said at the Capitol

On Monday, Oct. 19, 2026, State Auditor Tina Cannon hosted a transparency conference for local officials at the Utah State Capitol. She invited Dave Sanchez, director of the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission's Office of Municipal Securities, to speak about PIDs.

⚠️ The sales tax point

Utah's state development authorities can collect sales tax to repay bonds. Sanchez called that surprising, and said few other states allow it.

What the law says:

  • S.B. 316 (2025), primary sponsor Sen. Jerry W. Stevenson, House sponsor Rep. Val L. Peterson, deals with "the distribution of certain sales tax revenues related to certain authorities and zones."
  • The Inland Port Authority (UIPA) gets 20% of the local sales tax collected inside its project areas, beginning July 1, 2024.
  • The Point of the Mountain State Land Authority gets 50% of the state's 4.7% sales tax on sales on its land.
  • MIDA gets a share of sales tax in its project areas. Its board can also levy its own resort communities sales tax, energy tax, telecommunications tax and hotel-room tax (Utah Code 63H-1-203).
  • The Utah Lake Authority gets 50% of the local sales tax inside its boundary.

How many other states? No official count was found, and the SEC did not name any. In some states a local district can add its own sales tax to repay bonds: a Kansas community improvement district can add up to 2 cents on the dollar, a Missouri community improvement district up to 1%, and Colorado metro districts can levy a sales tax inside the district. The difference in Utah: the Legislature also hands a share of the regular sales tax to appointed state authorities.

What Sanchez said

  • PIDs have "real potential to become a problem if not handled correctly." The SEC is "agnostic" on whether they are good policy.
  • "These unelected boards have the ability to issue bonds, impose taxes and pass those costs onto taxpayers in your state." Being further removed from accountable officials creates "elevated risk."
  • "We can bring charges not just against the PID, but also against the creating entity." He asked officials to think about "control person liability": "If you have any ability to control that entity, you may be responsible for their securities law violations."
  • "Indirect risks may not be appreciated by folks making these policy decisions," and advisers or bond counsel may not explain them.
  • Recommending a PID amounts to recommending bonds, which can make that person a "municipal adviser" who must register with the SEC.

What State Auditor Tina Cannon said

"We hear from municipalities that they're being told ... 'this is what we want, and this is what we want you to use.' ... They feel like they have no choice."

PIDs are "one of the No. 1 topics for hotline complaints over misuse."

"You really have a hidden second mortgage payment."

On whether cheaper district financing reaches homebuyers and renters: "Nothing's impossible to measure. However, the reality is: it's not part of the discussion at this point in Utah."

The numbers from the Auditor's office

A correction to the news coverage: the Salt Lake Tribune reported that PIDs may levy $1.50 per $1,000 of value. The law says 0.015 per dollar, which is $15 per $1,000 (Utah Code 17D-4-303(1)). This page uses the law's number.

What the SEC did not say

No investigation of any Utah PID, city, county or developer was reported, and no violations were found. Sanchez said the SEC does not enforce PIDs' compliance with state law; it mainly regulates how governments communicate with investors. In a sample of Utah PIDs he reviewed, he found no conflict-of-interest disclosure problems. Coalville Mayor Rory Swensen called the conference "highly technical," with few "concrete answers."

Background: in August, Cannon told the Political Subdivisions Interim Committee the SEC had noticed developers issuing government bonds. Bond attorney Randy Larsen of GilmoreBell called that "the beauty of the tool," because it draws out-of-state capital to projects local banks could not finance, and said the development itself is the only collateral.

What UIPA and MIDA told lawmakers, checked against the record

Two days after the SEC visit, on Oct. 21, 2026, the Legislature's Economic Development and Workforce Services Interim Committee heard from the Utah Inland Port Authority (UIPA) and the Military Installation Development Authority (MIDA). Both can create PIDs, and both have appointed boards. MIDA helped create one of the state's first PIDs in 2020, for Extell's development near Park City (Salt Lake Tribune). These are the statements that touch PIDs and who pays.

UIPA · Executive Director Ben Hart
"No new taxes at all. It's future growth."
True, with context left out

No new tax rate is created. But UIPA keeps 75% of the growth in property tax that the county, schools and others would otherwise collect, for 25 years per parcel or up to 40. UIPA pledges its share to PIDs, and a PID can put assessments or a property tax on owners inside it. Tooele Valley's PID has issued $33 million in bonds. UIPA also receives 20% of local sales tax in its project areas (S.B. 316, 2025).

UIPA · Ben Hart
The money "stays in the project area."
True, with context left out

Mostly. Utah Code 11-58-602 also lets UIPA spend its share on its own overhead, on infrastructure outside a project area if its board decides it benefits the area (and the board's decision is final), and on housing "within or near" the area.

UIPA · presentation
A community has to invite UIPA in.
True, with context left out

True for the newer project areas. The first and largest, the Northwest Quadrant, was drawn by the Legislature in 2018, not by invitation.

MIDA · Executive Director Dan Hemmert
Counties keep control of land use.
Partly true

Utah Code 63H-1-202(2) says land in a MIDA project area is not subject to county or city land-use law or ordinances. In Box Elder, the county recommends review committee members, but the MIDA board appoints them and they "serve at the pleasure of the MIDA Board" (Agreement 26-15). Wasatch County's 2023 agreement says MIDA keeps "full and exclusive jurisdiction, including all land use authority."

MIDA · Dan Hemmert
If a project finishes early, a "waterfall" returns the money to all taxing entities.
Not supported by records found

We did not find that clause in state law or the Box Elder agreement. State law lets MIDA use the money "before, during, and after" the collection period. (Falcon Hill and Wasatch East Side agreements not yet checked.)

MIDA · Dan Hemmert
"We get zero state money."
Not supported by records found

MIDA's own Sept. 18, 2023 presentation to the Legislature lists $71,194,600 in legislative appropriations for fiscal years 2022 through 2024. We did not find whether MIDA received state money this year.

MIDA · notice
Posting meetings on MIDA's website is enough notice.
What was said

Rep. James Dunnigan told Hemmert website posting is "not adequate notice to the public." Hemmert answered that a local notice requirement "might be a great modification." State law says MIDA is "not required to physically post notice."

A PID created by MIDA can tax at a higher limit than a regular PID.
Matches the record

Utah Code 63H-1-202(10): a MIDA-created PID may levy up to 0.015 for operations and maintenance, and a separate bond levy up to 0.02. A regular PID is limited to 0.015 for all purposes, including limited-tax bonds (17D-4-303).

When a project fails, and closer to home

Wohali, Coalville (Summit County)

Wohali Land Estates, a golf resort development, filed Chapter 11 bankruptcy on Aug. 8, 2025. Its PID had issued almost $35 million in debt. To avoid foreclosure on a $3.3 million PID payment, the developer needed a court-approved loan of more than $4 million. Landowners stepped in with about $160,000 to cover payroll. Utah law says a PID's debts are not the city's, county's or state's (17D-4-201(6)(c)). The State Auditor began raising concerns about PIDs after this bankruptcy.

How much do builders save? What the records show, and an estimate

There are three different numbers here, and they shouldn't be mixed up: what the district borrowed and spent (a record), how much cheaper its interest is (an estimate), and what taxpayers in the district will repay (depends on the bond schedule).

What the record shows: about $28.4 million of public-district borrowing is set aside to pay the developer back for infrastructure it builds. Repayment falls on the land in the district, not on the developer as a borrower. That reimbursement is the developer's biggest benefit, bigger than the interest rate.

3. What taxpayers in the district repay

Interest on the full $39.38 million at 6.5% is about $2.56 million a year. How much is repaid in total depends on when principal comes due, and that schedule was not in the budget we read:

  • If principal were repaid evenly over 30 years (an assumption): about $3.0 million a year, about $90 million in all.
  • If principal were repaid at the end in 2055 (another assumption): about $77 million in interest plus the $39.38 million, about $117 million in all.

Either way, it is paid through the district's levy by whoever owns the land. Once homes sell, that's the homeowners. Statewide, a 3.65-point gap on $5.3 billion of PID and similar debt would be roughly $190 million a year; PID rates vary, so this is only a size estimate.

State Auditor Tina Cannon on PID bonds: "for a developer, this is a gift," because developers can "pass that cost on in 30 to 40 years of additional tax payments" (Utah Investigative Journalism Project, April 28, 2026). State Treasurer Marlo Oaks gave a hypothetical: PID taxes adding $50,000 over decades to a $450,000 home. Bond attorney Randy Larsen of GilmoreBell has said the development itself is the only collateral and no local government is responsible for repayment (KPCW, Aug. 25, 2026).

Does the developer still benefit?

Yes. District bonds are usually cheaper money than a private construction loan, and the developer pays each lot's share when that lot sells, out of the sale. What changes: unsold lots stay the developer's bill, so expanding only pays off if the new lots will sell. Fair caveat: the cost moves into the home price, so buyers still pay it, once, up front, and knowingly.

The reform, in four parts

  1. Payoff at first sale. For new PIDs, every home's assessment is paid in full before it goes to the person who will live there (the Tech Ridge rule). Apartments pay off within a set time after opening (the Sawmill rule). Utah already requires this for IFD homes (H.B. 13, 2024; now Utah Code 17D-5-102(2)(d)).
  2. Fallback: disclosure first. If part 1 stalls, bring back the early H.B. 422 rule: a written PID notice, and a buyer who doesn't get it can walk away and keep their earnest money.
  3. A hearing on big annexations. Above a set size, the city or county that created the district holds a public hearing first.
  4. Everything tied to the land, paid off up front. When a lot is sold, the developer pays off everything tied to that land in full: its share of the PID debt and its share of any tax increment pledged to the project (a CRA, TIF, Inland Port or MIDA project area). Once a lot is paid off, its property tax goes back in full to the general funds of the county, schools, fire and water districts that serve it, instead of being held back for 15, 20 or 25 years.
This is a proposal built on the records above. It is clearly labeled as the Hive's proposal, separate from the records themselves.

Words to know

Certified tax rate
The rate that would bring in the same property tax as last year, plus new growth. Going above it requires a Truth in Taxation notice and hearing.
Assessment
A set dollar charge on each lot to repay district bonds, paid in yearly installments. It can be paid off.
Renewal (extension)
Keeping a CRA, Inland Port or MIDA collection going past its end date, or refinancing PID bonds so the levy runs longer. For a CRA, the taxing bodies must approve after a public hearing. For the Inland Port and MIDA, their own boards can add up to 15 years.
Refunding bonds
New bonds issued to pay off old ones, like refinancing a mortgage. A PID's levy continues until any refunding bonds are paid too.
Mill levy
A tax rate. One mill is $1 per $1,000 of taxable value. A Utah primary home is taxed on 55% of its value.
Tax increment (TIF, CRA)
The growth in property tax in an area, captured for years by a project instead of going to the county, schools and other local governments.
IFD (infrastructure financing district)
A newer cousin of the PID, created in 2024 under a different part of state law (17D-5). IFD homes must be paid off before anyone moves in.
Municipal adviser
Under federal law, someone who advises a government on bonds. The SEC says recommending a PID can count, and municipal advisers must register.
Control person liability
Under securities law, a person or body that controls an entity can be responsible for that entity's violations. The SEC raised it about cities and counties that create PIDs.
