What the New Boards Inherit

Alpine School District splits in three on July 1, 2027. The boards that take delivery were seated in January 2026, and two things were already waiting for them: revenue committed away for decades, and $201 million of debt issued without an election. Both were made possible by a law passed in 2025. Not one legislator voted against it.

Utah County · Schools & Bonding · Published September 2026

The sequence

Everything on this page is drawn from bill files, committee minutes, adopted budgets, and published election results. What follows is the order in which the documents say things happened. It is a sequence, not an explanation — nothing here establishes that any step caused any other, and this page does not claim that it does.

Two inheritances, neither on a ballot

Voters approved Propositions 11 and 14 in November 2024. The Utah County Commission set boundaries in March 2025. New boards were elected that November and took office in January 2026. Each new district sets its own property tax rate beginning FY2027–28.

The three successor districts: Aspen Peaks, Lake Mountain (Eagle Mountain, Saratoga Springs, Cedar Fort, Fairfield), and Timpanogos (Orem, Pleasant Grove, Lindon, Vineyard).

The debt: what S.B. 188 changed

Utah school districts borrow two ways. A general obligation bond goes on the November ballot. A lease revenue bond does not — it is issued through a local building authority, in practice the same school board sitting as a second body, and approved by resolution after a public hearing.

Utah law capped lease revenue bonds at a combined $200 million over any three-year period without voter approval. S.B. 188 of the 2025 General Session removed that cap for reorganized districts. Its primary sponsor was Sen. Keith Grover.

Where the exemption entered the bill

It was not in the introduced bill or in either Senate substitute. It was added in the House Education Standing Committee on February 27, 2025, as part of Amendment #2, moved by Rep. Matt MacPherson and adopted 12–0–4. The committee minutes reproduce the language:

"(ii) A lease revenue bond described in Subsection (5)(c)(i) is not subject to the combined total limitation described in Subsection 11-14-103(6)(b)(i) due to the prior approval of voters within the new or reorganized new school district for the general obligation bond."

House Education Standing Committee minutes, February 27, 2025 — Amendment #2, item 5

The provision immediately preceding it sets the trigger: it applies where, within the preceding three years, "voters within the divided school district rejected a general obligation bond."

The bill's own stated rationale is written into that text — that voters within the sub-area had approved the bond even where the district as a whole had not. That is the argument on the record, and readers should weigh it.

What Alpine did with it

$201,000,000

brought to market in July 2025, of up to $238 million approved — for a high school in Saratoga Springs and an elementary in Eagle Mountain. Both cities fall in Lake Mountain District. Terms run up to 31 years.

No election was held. The debt is to be assumed by the successor district after the 2027 split.

Stated fairly

Lease revenue bonds are a lawful, long-established instrument, and districts use them because schools are often needed sooner than an election cycle permits. Saratoga Springs and Eagle Mountain are among the fastest-growing cities in Utah, and the need for those buildings is not in question. What changed in 2025 was the ceiling, and only for districts in Alpine's circumstances.

The disclosure Utah law requires on these notices

Utah's public notice statute anticipates the use of this instrument after a failed bond election. Lease revenue bond notices carry a required disclosure for when proceeds will fund a project whose general obligation financing voters previously rejected, alongside a statutory acknowledgment that a lease revenue bond generally costs more in interest than a voter-approved general obligation bond.

North Summit School District's December 2025 notice for up to $125 million carried that line. We have not established whether any Alpine notice did, and we do not assert either way.

Serving two boards at once

The dual-role question has already been reported locally. The Lehi Free Press and American Fork Citizen examined it on July 13, 2026, identifying officials who simultaneously hold seats on the dissolving Alpine board and on a successor board while assets and liabilities are divided: Julie King (Alpine board president and Lake Mountain board president), Ada Wilson (Alpine Seat 7 and Timpanogos Seat 4), Joylin Lincoln (Alpine and Lake Mountain Seat 7), and Nicki Brammer (Aspen Peaks).

King has addressed it publicly, saying she has not recused herself because she has not participated on both sides of the same decision-making body. Alpine board member Sarah Beeson raised the concern, and a third-party reviewer, Burke Jolley, audited the asset-and-liability framework before approval.

What this page adds

That reporting did not address bonds, debt, tax increment, or dollar figures. The financial inheritance described here has not previously been connected to it.

One of those seats sits inside a political funding organization

Nicki Brammer holds Seat 3 on the Aspen Peaks board, won in November 2025 with 55.4% against Mary Thomas. She is also Board Chair for Utah County of the Doers Network, a self-described conservative organization that endorses and funds candidates. Its own website publishes both facts together, describing her as having "recently elected to the Aspen Peaks School Board." Readers can see the organization's own materials, including its endorsement list, at doersnetwork.org.

In 2026 the Doers Network made roughly $70,300 in documented contributions to Utah candidates and committees. Two recipients appear elsewhere on this page: Rep. Stephanie Gricius, who presented S.B. 188 in House Education, and Rep. Matt MacPherson, who moved the amendment containing the bond cap exemption. A third is Sen. Brady Brammer, who represents Senate District 21 and is Nicki Brammer's husband.

So one household sits at three points of the same structure at once: a seat on a successor school board, the county chairmanship of an organization that funds legislators, and a seat in the Legislature that receives that organization's money.

The timing matters, and it cuts against the obvious reading

Every Doers Network contribution identified is from 2026. S.B. 188 was heard and passed in February and March 2025. The money postdates the bill by roughly a year. Nothing in the record suggests the organization funded the legislation's path, and this page does not suggest it.

What the record does show is narrower: an organization that funds legislators in these districts has, as its Utah County board chair, a sitting member of one of the successor school boards that inherits the results.

Two further points belong here for accuracy. No Doers Network contribution to Nicki Brammer's own school board campaign appears in her filings. Her August 2025 report shows $3,550 raised in total — a $1,500 loan from herself, $2,000 from Brady Brammer, and $50 from Rep. Tyler Clancy. And the organization's own funding sources are not established in any record we reviewed; contributions out are documented, contributions in are not.

An unresolved figure

Records reviewed give conflicting totals for the Doers Network's contributions to Sen. Brady Brammer — $2,000 in one compilation, $7,000 across two payments in another. That discrepancy has not been resolved, and neither figure should be treated as final.

The revenue: what the county said, and what the budgets say

Utah County's public materials on the split state that it "isn't expected to significantly impact tax collection or school funding," citing per-pupil WPU funding as the stabilizer.

WPU is state money, distributed per student. Tax increment operates on the local property tax base.

New growth is the one increase that arrives without a public hearing. Under Utah's Truth in Taxation law, a taxing entity's certified rate is adjusted downward as existing property values rise, so rising valuations do not by themselves produce more revenue. The exception is new construction added to the rolls — the one source of additional revenue a district receives automatically.

Tax increment captures that exception. The one revenue increase a district receives without asking is the one committed to the project.

The shortfall does not disappear — it moves. To recover it, a district's options are a Truth in Taxation rate increase, which falls on every property owner in the district including those nowhere near the project, or reduced spending.

None of which makes the county's statement inaccurate about WPU. It answers a different question than the one the adopted budgets raise.

What was already committed

The mechanism, in full, on another page

How the freeze works, why the school district is always the largest contributor, and the complete Sweetwater Industrial Park CRA #3 ledger — base year, rate stack, and the twenty-year split — are set out in Where Does Your School-Tax Dollar Go?

In short: inside Sweetwater CRA #3 in Eagle Mountain, Alpine School District's levy is projected to generate $20,906,417 of increment over twenty years. Of that, $13,518,614 is committed to the project and $7,387,803 retained. What returns to the district is $500,000, through a School District Pilot Program line.

Eagle Mountain is in Lake Mountain District — as is the new high school in Saratoga Springs and the new elementary in Eagle Mountain that the $201 million was issued to build.

The denominator

Nothing above changes on July 1, 2027. The agreements are the agreements. What changes is the size of the district absorbing them. Alpine serves roughly 84,000 students; Lake Mountain will be built from four cities.

The reportable finding is a count, not a claim

Nothing here asserts that any successor district inherits five project areas, or ten, or twenty. No such count has been published. The individual project area budgets are public, filed city by city on the Utah Public Notice Website. What does not exist is a tally of how many fall inside each new boundary and what they total.

That is the most useful work left in this story, and it is arithmetic: list the project areas inside each successor district, add the committed totals, and set the sum against that district's own budget alongside its assumed debt service. Until someone does, the exposure is unknown — which is itself worth reporting, given that the boards inheriting it took office in January.

Questions we can answer here

  • Did anyone vote on the $201 million? No election was held. Lease revenue bonds are approved by board resolution after a public hearing. S.B. 188 removed the $200 million three-year cap that had applied.
  • Where did the exemption come from? House Education Standing Committee, February 27, 2025, Amendment #2, moved by Rep. Matt MacPherson, adopted 12–0–4. It was not in the introduced bill.
  • Did anyone vote against the bill? No. Every recorded vote at every stage was unanimous. The changes to its content were made by voice vote.
  • Which successor district assumes the debt? The buildings are in Saratoga Springs and Eagle Mountain, both in Lake Mountain District. Terms run up to 31 years.
  • Does the split reduce the amount of revenue committed? No. The agreements are unchanged by the split. Only the size of the district absorbing them changes.
  • Is one project area enough to damage a district's budget? On its own, no — under a third of one percent on the illustrative figures. The open question is how many there are.

Questions you need to ask

  • How many project areas fall inside each successor district's boundary, and what do their committed totals come to?
  • What is each successor district's projected annual budget and annual debt service?
  • What did the conference committee change on March 7, and who served on it?
  • Were the new boards briefed on the revenue commitments and the assumed bond debt, and when?
  • Was either inheritance disclosed in the Proposition 11 and 14 ballot materials in 2024?
  • Did Alpine's lease revenue bond notices carry the statutory disclosure regarding previously rejected general obligation financing?
  • What reason was given, on the record, for lifting the cap for reorganized districts?
  • Who funds the Doers Network? Its contributions out are documented; its contributions in are not.
  • Does a successor board member who chairs a political funding organization's county board face any recusal obligation when that organization funds legislators whose decisions affect the district?

How the calculations were made

Per job. $13,518,614 ÷ 120 = $112,655; ÷ 150 = $90,124. Return: $500,000 ÷ 120 = $4,167; ÷ 150 = $3,333. Ratio $13,518,614 ÷ $500,000 = 27.0.

Committed share. $13,518,614 ÷ $20,906,417 = 64.7%; $7,387,803 retained. Return share: $500,000 ÷ $13,518,614 = 3.7%.

Annual average. $13,518,614 ÷ 20 = $675,931, rounded to $675,900. An even division for scale, not a projection of any single year.

Illustrative tables. Budget figures and the $85,000 per-position cost are invented placeholders, labeled as such wherever they appear.
