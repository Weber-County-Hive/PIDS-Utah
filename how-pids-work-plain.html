How PIDs Work, in Plain Words

Posted Oct. 29, 2026 · Updated Oct. 29, 2026

Read the full version, with every source →

What is a PID?

A PID (Public Infrastructure District) is a small government district. It borrows money to build roads, water lines and sewer for a new neighborhood. The people who own the homes later pay that money back, through a yearly bill on the house.

It starts on empty land. The developer usually owns all of it, so the developer is the one who says yes. The board that runs it is appointed, not elected, at first.

How does it grow?

  1. The district's starting papers draw a bigger map for later.
  2. The developer buys or owns more land inside that map and says yes for it.
  3. The district's board adds the land. No new city or county vote is needed.
  4. New land, new bills, maybe new borrowing. Then it can happen again.

Who pays?

The bill rides with the house. When you sell, your buyer takes it over, then their buyer, for decades. Utah law does not make the seller pay it off. It only says the buyer must be shown the yearly cost at or before closing. If you learn about it that late and back out, you can lose your earnest money.

The developer got the roads and pipes. The homeowner carries the bill, and so does the next owner.

What families already pay, before any PID

$529
a year. That's what 15 property-tax increases since 2022 add to the bill on a typical ($434,583) Weber County home. This is not a PID bill. It's what the schools, fire district, county, sewer and water districts added, and everyone in those districts pays it. But it's connected: when a growth deal sends the new property-tax money to a project for years, the schools, fire and county still have to serve those new homes without it. When the money isn't coming in, they have to make up the difference, with higher rates on everyone else or fewer services. If you buy inside a PID, you pay this plus the PID's own bill. A 5-mill PID, like Nordic Village's, would add about $1,195 a year more on the same home. City increases, utility fees and the county's new sales tax aren't in the $529 either, so the real total is higher.

Biggest parts: Weber School District, about $319 a year. Weber Fire District, about $94. Weber County, about $47. Central Weber Sewer, about $39. Weber Basin Water, about $29.

We used the state's own tax rates and counted only increases that actually happened. An earlier version said $255. It was off, so we fixed it.

Property tax budgets nearly doubled since 2018

+92%
Nine Weber County taxing bodies (the schools, county, fire, sewer, water, 911 and Ogden City) budgeted $186.6 million in property tax for 2018 and $358.8 million for 2026.

At the same time, about $12 million to $14 million a year of new property-tax growth went to development project areas instead of to them, $62 million from 2020 to 2024. MIDA's Falcon Hill area alone went from $0 to $2.87 million a year.

For Weber County, the money held back in 2024 was close to the size of its 2024 tax increase. For Weber School District, it was about a third of its 2025 increase. The records don't prove the held-back money caused the increases (it's about 4% of the budgets), but these bodies serve the new homes and businesses without that money. Bigger new deals, like Nordic Village and the Inland Port, are just starting.

What builders save (an estimate)

From the record: Nordic Village's district borrowed $39.38 million at 6.5%. About $28.4 million of it is set aside to pay the developer back for roads, water and sewer it builds; the rest went to interest, reserves and fees. People who own land in the district repay it through 2055.

Estimate: if a private loan cost about 10%, the district's rate saves roughly $1 million a year in interest on that $28.4 million.

How long do they last? And what is "renewing"?

Renewing (an extension) means keeping the money flowing past the end date. Who decides is different for each one:

  • Nordic Village PID: its bonds run to 2055. The tax stays until the bonds are paid, and refinancing can push that out. Its appointed board decides.
  • Nordic Village CRA: 15 years. To extend it, there has to be a public hearing, and the county, school, fire and water boards each have to say yes.
  • Inland Port: 25 years for each piece of land, starting when that land starts paying. Its own board can add 15 more years. The county, schools and fire districts don't get a vote.
  • MIDA: the same: 25 years per piece of land, plus up to 15 more if MIDA's board says so.

What federal regulators said at the Capitol

On Oct. 19, 2026, the State Auditor invited a top official from the SEC (the federal agency that watches over bonds and investments) to speak to local leaders about PIDs.

⚠️ Sales tax: in Utah, some state development agencies, like the Inland Port and MIDA, get a share of the sales tax you pay at the register to help pay off their bonds. The SEC official called that surprising, and said few other states allow it. A 2025 law, S.B. 316, sets those shares.
  • He said PIDs have "real potential to become a problem if not handled correctly."
  • He said the unelected boards can borrow, tax, and pass those costs to taxpayers.
  • He said a city or county that controls a PID could be held responsible for its securities problems.
  • He did not say any Utah district broke the law, and no investigation was reported.

What the State Auditor said

Cities tell her developers say, in effect, "this is what we want you to use," and "they feel like they have no choice."

PIDs are "one of the No. 1 topics" for complaints to her hotline.

For a homeowner, "you really have a hidden second mortgage payment."

Fewer than half of the districts follow the state's openness rules. About 250 of them have borrowed $5.3 billion so far.

What the Inland Port and MIDA told lawmakers

Two days later, on Oct. 21, both agencies spoke to a legislative committee. Both can create PIDs. Here's what we checked:

  • Inland Port: "No new taxes." No new tax rate, true. But it keeps 75% of the growth in property tax that schools and the county would get, for 25 years or more, and it backs PIDs that do bill owners.
  • MIDA: counties keep control of land. State law says land in a MIDA area isn't under county land-use rules, and MIDA's board picks the review committee.
  • MIDA: "We get zero state money." MIDA's own 2023 report to the Legislature lists $71.2 million in state money for 2022 to 2024.
  • MIDA's PIDs can tax more: up to $15 per $1,000 for upkeep, plus up to $20 per $1,000 for bonds. A regular PID tops out at $15 for most purposes.

A fix already working in Utah

Three Utah districts already make the bill get paid off before a family moves in: Tech Ridge in St. George, Firefly in Eagle Mountain and Sawmill in Wasatch County. The developer still gets cheaper money to build. The cost goes into the price once, up front, where the buyer can see it.

The reform: (1) make that payoff the rule for new PIDs, (2) if that stalls, make sellers give buyers a written PID notice before earnest money, (3) hold a public hearing before a district adds a lot of land, and (4) when a lot is sold, the developer pays off everything tied to that land, the PID debt and its share of any growth-deal money, so the full property tax goes back to the schools, fire, county and water districts right away instead of 15 to 25 years later.

Questions you can ask

  • Your legislators, city and county: Why is public money paying for private development when the people who pay it back never got a say?
  • Your state legislators: Will you carry a bill so PID homes are paid off before a family moves in?
  • Your city or county: Can our PID add land without coming back to you?
  • Before you buy: Is this home in a PID? What's the yearly cost? What's still owed?
