What prompted this

The post

On Oct. 26, 2026, Sen. John Curtis posted on X that Utah's housing shortage keeps pushing up costs, that building near existing transit can add housing "without adding to traffic," and that the Build HUBS Act, by "cutting some burdensome red tape," can help Utah families afford to stay.

The Build HUBS Act is S. 3636, introduced Jan. 14, 2026 by Sen. Lisa Blunt Rochester (D-Del.) for herself and Sen. Curtis (R-Utah), with a House companion, H.R. 7062. This page reads the bill and checks each claim in the post against public records, including Utah's own transit-zone projects that the Hive has already documented.

Where the bill stands

It is not law

Senate

S. 3636 was introduced Jan. 14, 2026 and referred to the Senate Committee on Environment and Public Works. No hearing, committee vote or floor vote is listed.

House

The House companion is H.R. 7062.

The path to watch

Supporters are asking Congress to fold the bill into the next federal surface transportation bill. That is how it could become law without a stand-alone vote.

What the monthly payment eats

Gardner's estimated payment on a median-priced home is $3,669 a month ($44,028 a year). That is 45.6% of the Gardner median household income of $96,658, and 68.8% of the median renter income of $64,000. The common lending guide is 30%. Gardner puts the income needed at $146,800, which is $50,142 (52%) more than the median household earns.

S. 3636 in plain words

What the Build HUBS Act does, step by step

1

The government has a big lending piggy bank

Washington keeps money it lends out to build roads and train tracks. People who borrow it have to pay it back, plus a little extra called interest.

2

This bill lets builders borrow it for homes and stores

If they build within a half mile of a train or bus station (about a 10-minute walk), builders could borrow from that piggy bank to put up apartments, homes and shops.

3

Some homes have to cost less to get a discount

If more than half of the homes are priced for families who earn less than most of their neighbors (80% of the area's middle income or less), the builder pays only half the normal interest.

4

But the bill doesn't say for how long

Nothing in the bill says those homes must stay cheaper for 5 years, 30 years, or any number of years. No promise is written on the property.

5

Builders with no cheaper homes can still borrow

They just don't get the half-price interest. A project near a station can use the loans even if every home is full price.

6

Some checking gets skipped

Usually the government checks how a project affects nature and neighbors. The bill skips that check when an old office is turned into homes, or when building on land already used for roads or trains.

7

Your town still makes the rules

The bill says it does not change your city's or county's rules about what can be built where.

The short version

Who is cheering for it: home builders, Realtors, apartment owners and city planning groups. What it gives builders: cheaper loans and less paperwork near stations. What it doesn't promise families: that the cheaper homes stay cheaper.

Read the bill

What S. 3636 actually does

  • Extends two federal loan programs (TIFIA for transportation, RRIF for railroads) for fiscal years 2027–2031 and lets them lend for transit-oriented development within a half mile of a rail, bus rapid transit or multimodal station. No dollar amount is written into the bill.
  • Defines "attainable housing" as serving households up to 120% of area median income, with a majority of units affordable at or below 80%. No time limit, no deed restriction in the text.
  • Half-price interest: attainable housing loans are set at one-half the Treasury rate when the loan is signed.
  • Lower credit bar: TIFIA projects "shall not be required to have an investment-grade rating." Private lenders approved as "originator-servicers" can underwrite the loans for the government, modeled on HUD's multifamily system.
  • NEPA exclusions for office conversions and building on already-disturbed transportation land.
  • No zoning takeover: Section 5 says nothing in the Act preempts state or local zoning.
  • Backers named on the sponsors' one-pager include the National Association of Home Builders, the National Association of REALTORS, the National Apartment Association, The Real Estate Roundtable and planning groups. Utah Transit Authority board chair Carlton Christensen is quoted in support.

What the bill requires, and what it leaves out

Requires: the half rate only for projects where most units are at or below 80% of area median income; coordination with the regional planning organization; federal labor standards; local zoning stays local. Leaves out: any time limit or deed restriction on "attainable" units, any affordable share for ordinary transit-project loans, and any public report of which projects get loans.

Bottom line from the record

The bill can add homes near transit with cheaper federal loans. Whether those homes stay within reach of the families the label names depends on rules the bill leaves out: how long units stay affordable and who reports on them. Utah's own transit zones show what happens when those rules are loose.

What the record shows about the "fixes"

Three bills get pointed to as protections. H.B. 507's PID disclosure can come at or before closing, after a buyer's earnest money is already down. H.B. 37's deed restrictions apply only where a city or county opts into its density bonus. S.B. 23 (2025) changed FHIZ density and affordability rules but kept the captured-tax-growth method in place. The bills that would have shown the public the cost up front (H.B. 422) or lowered homeowners' property taxes (HJR 7) did not pass.

Not just Utah

How many other states this reaches

Build HUBS

It is a federal bill, so it would apply in every state with rail, bus rapid transit or multimodal stations that qualify for TIFIA or RRIF loans.

Tax increment financing

Every state except Arizona, plus Washington, D.C., has a law allowing tax increment financing. Arizona repealed its TIF law in 1999.

Special districts on new homes

The same homeowner-assessment model is used in other states under other names, such as Public Improvement Districts and Municipal Utility Districts in Texas and Community Development Districts in Florida.

The TIF count comes from a secondary summary (Wikipedia, citing state enabling laws); the special-district examples from RCLCO's review of master-planned community financing.

Words in this file

Glossary (tap a word)

TIFIA

A federal loan program for transportation projects. The bill would let it lend for homes and shops near stations.

RRIF

A federal loan program for railroads and rail stations, changed the same way.

NEPA

The National Environmental Policy Act. It requires federal review of how a federally funded project affects the environment.

Categorical exclusion

A type of project the government decides can skip the full NEPA review.

AMI

Area median income. Half the households in an area earn more, half earn less. HUD sets it each year.

Treasury rate

The interest rate the U.S. government pays to borrow. TIFIA loans are normally priced near it.

Transit-oriented development (TOD)

Homes, shops and offices built close to a train or bus rapid transit station.

HTRZ

Housing and Transit Reinvestment Zone, a Utah tool (2021) that lets a project near a station keep most of its new property tax growth.

PID

Public Infrastructure District. Homeowners inside it pay a yearly assessment that repays bonds for roads, pipes and other infrastructure.

Deed restriction

A promise written on the property records that keeps a home affordable for a set number of years, even after it sells.
