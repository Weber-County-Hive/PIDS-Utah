$7,500 a Home: Who Gets the West Weber Parks Money?

When farmland in West Weber is rezoned for houses, builders give $7,500 a home for parks. In 2024 the county said that money belonged to the park districts. In 2026, county staff said it belongs to the county. The same records show how a park BlackPine gave away ended up inside an Inland Port area, and how much public money is behind this growth.

Where
Farm country west of Ogden, outside any city. The Promontory Commerce Center business park is near 5900 West and 900 South. The Bennet neighborhood is planned on the Gibson farm, between 4700 West, 1150 South and the Weber River.
Who
The Weber County Commission and its staff. BlackPine, the developer, and its partners. The board that runs the business park's PID. The Utah Inland Port Authority. Two volunteer park boards: West Warren and Taylor West Weber.

Short version
  • The $7,500: when farmland in West Weber gets rezoned for houses, builders have agreed to give $7,500 for each new home. In 2024 the county said this money goes to the park districts, and the park districts decide how to spend it.
  • What changed: on Nov. 2, 2026, county staff said the money is "a donation to Weber County," and the county decides. Commissioner Gage Froerer said he wants it to pay for roads too. No vote was taken.
  • The park and the port: in 2023, BlackPine gave land for a park to the West Warren park district. A few months later, the commission moved BlackPine's business park into an Inland Port area. That sends most of the business park's new tax money to the port, not the park district.
  • Who runs the PID: two BlackPine partners, Daniel Stephens and Garrett Hansen, run the board of the business park's PID. They said so on sworn forms.
  • The public's money: by our estimate from the port's own plan, about $360.8 million in local property tax growth goes to the port over 25 years instead of local budgets. That's about $206.5 million from the school district's share and $5.2–5.8 million from the park's. Federal relief money and a port loan, $5 million each, help pay for the sewer.

Why aren't data centers paying?
A data center would pay full property tax. But for 25 years, 75% of its growth would go to the port, not to schools or parks. The 2024 plan also offered big businesses 10% to 30% of their own tax growth back. The port says it isn't giving data centers deals now, and H.B. 507 sets new rules. The sewer such a business could use was paid for partly with federal and port money.
What the $7,500 is

In 2024, the county said it was the park districts' money

When a builder wants farmland rezoned for more houses, the county and the builder sign a contract. Several West Weber contracts we read say the builder gives $7,500 for each lot or home, for parks. The contracts call it a voluntary gift, "not a fee or exaction." But the same contract says the county won't take any building or planning applications until the park district says it got paid.

Winston Park contract, section 9.6.1 (county commission packet, July 2024)
"Developer agrees to donate $7,500 per lot or unit within the Project to the Park District. Developer agrees that this is a donation offered of the Developer's own free will and is not a fee or exaction imposed by the County or Park District. Developer also agrees that once this donation is made the Park District has full discretion on how to use the donation… No Building Division or Planning Division application will be accepted or approved in the Project until the County receives written confirmation of this donation from the Park District."
  1. Dec. 5, 2023
    A rezone passes 3-0. The vote says the park money must "be sent to the Park District." Back then it was $2,000 a lot.
  2. Mar. 12, 2024
    County planners meet with the park boards about how much parks need. West Warren's board explains how it came up with "$7,500 per lot." Planners say the commission wants it to be the same for everyone.
  3. Mar. 26, 2024
    Longhorn Estates rezone passes 3-0. Staff tell the commission the builder will pay $7,500 a lot "to the parks District."
  4. June 11, 2024
    Winston Park hearing. Staff say parks cost about "$7.5k per lot." The commission chair says: "that was not a decision by this legislative body but a decision by the park district legislative body." Staff: "the parks district derived the number."
  5. July 11, 2024
    A planning memo says Winston Park's contract now requires $7,500 a lot, "as requested by the Commission."
  6. Nov. 2, 2026

We searched the commission's minutes from December 2023 through July 2024 and didn't find a county law setting the $7,500. The records describe it three ways: the park districts' number (June 2024), something the commission asked for (July 2024), and an "ordinance," or county law (Froerer, Nov. 2, 2026).

Two pots of money

The business park's roads already have money

Pot 1 · The business park

PID loans: $25.9 million borrowed in 2026. The business park's land pays it back. It's for roads, water, sewer and storm drains.

Port tax money: for 25 years, 75% of the new tax growth stays with the port. The port's May 20, 2024 minutes say that money must go to "public infrastructure - road, sewer, water." On Jan. 2, 2024, Froerer said port money goes "to infrastructure and hopefully to UDOT."

Pot 2 · The new homes

The $7,500 per home from rezones like Bennet, Winston Park and Longhorn Estates. The 2024 contracts send it to the park district for parks and trails.

This is the money Froerer wants to open up for roads.

Most new homes are outside the port area, so roads near the homes may not be paid for by port or PID money. On Nov. 2, Froerer didn't say which roads he meant.

What about the businesses?
The business park pays none of the $7,500, because it's charged per home. Businesses pay full property tax, but for 25 years, 75% of the growth goes to the port instead of local budgets like the park district's. That leaves the parks with their base share, 25% of the growth, and whatever the new homes donate.Source: Draft West Weber Project Area Plan, Jan. 10, 2024, p. 14 (75% to the project area, 25% to taxing entities, 25 years).

Common questions

Does it count condos, townhouses and apartments?
In the Winston Park contract, yes. It says "$7,500 per lot or unit." Other contracts may say it differently, and we haven't checked them all. The Bennet contract has no $7,500 rule that we found.
Why would a builder agree to it?
It's a deal made as part of the rezone, which lets the builder put more homes on the land. The contracts call it voluntary and "not a fee or exaction." But the county decides the rezone, and the Winston Park contract says no applications move forward until the park district says it got paid. A July 2024 planning memo says if the builder doesn't sign, "the rezone approval will not be valid."
How is this different from an impact fee?
An impact fee is set under a state law, the Utah Impact Fees Act. The government has to write a plan and a study showing what new growth will cost, hold a public hearing, and adopt the fee for everyone. The money can only pay for the improvements listed in that plan, and it generally has to be spent within six years or given back (Utah Code 11-36a-602 and 11-36a-603). The $7,500 is different. It's written into each builder's contract one at a time and called a voluntary gift. No study set the amount. It can be paid in land instead of cash. And under the 2024 contracts, the park district can spend it however it chooses, with no deadline and no refund rule in the contract.
Who pays it in the end?
Commissioner Froerer said on the record, Nov. 2, 2026: "Let's be real that 7500 goes on the backs of the prospective homeowner. It doesn't go to the developer." The commission has said in public that buyers carry the cost.

Taxed as farmland for now
Most of Bennet LLC's land is still taxed as farmland. Its county market value is $30.3 million, but its taxable value is about $3.4 million. That's typical of Utah's greenbelt (farmland) status, which lowers taxes while land is farmed. It means the land pays little tax now while it's being readied for up to 3,300 homes.Source: Weber County Assessor, 2026 assessed values (39 Bennet LLC parcels), pulled Nov. 9, 2026.
Who pays for the growth?
The records show four ways the cost lands on families:
  1. Business growth goes to the port. For 25 years, 75% of the new business tax growth stays with the port for roads and sewer. The park districts don't get that share (port draft plan, p. 14).
  2. The park districts have one tool. Their money comes from property tax. On Nov. 2, Froerer said park districts "have the right to go to the public and raise taxes." That would fall on people who already live there.
  3. The $7,500 rides on the house. Froerer on Nov. 2: it "goes on the backs of the prospective homeowner."
  4. PIDs bill the land. PID loans are paid back by charges on the land inside the PID. So far that's only the business park's land. The Bennet contract gives up the right to protest any future special district or charge "on behalf of itself and all future owners" (section 10.6.3). We found no record of a PID on Bennet's land.

Meanwhile, the farm is taxed as farmland while it gets ready for homes. Lawmakers and commissioners chose these rules. The records show how they work out.

How we figured this
The totals ($481.1 million of growth; $360.8 million to the port; $120.3 million kept locally) and each tax rate come from the port's draft plan, Appendix D, p. 38. We checked where the land is. The plan lists 96 parcels. We found 90 of them, about 9,009 acres, in the county's 2026 assessor data. All of those but one 1-acre parcel are inside the West Warren park district, so the park's tax covers the whole area. The park range uses the plan's 2023 total tax rate for the high end and the county's 2026 rates for these areas (1.008% to 1.079%) for the low end. These are estimates from a plan the port says will change. They aren't bills or audited amounts.

To know, the ARPA payments, loan payments and bond payments would have to be matched to the same bills. A county permit contract for the pump station, posted unsigned, says the ARPA money would stand in for the usual construction bond.

Land, water, data centers and the law

What the plans allow, and what changed in 2026

The land

The port says its West Weber area is about 9,000 acres in two parts, Little Mountain and Weber Bend, "near the eastern shore of the Great Salt Lake." The plan sets aside at least 1% of the port's money for wetlands (p. 13); county staff said 3% on Nov. 2, 2026. In December 2024 the commission changed the 300-foot buffer along the Weber River at BlackPine's request.

Data centers in the 2024 plan

The port's January 2024 plan lists the businesses its deals "will generally favor": advanced manufacturing, aerospace and defense, distribution and logistics, data centers, and renewable energy. It also offers big new businesses some of their own tax growth back: 10% for $25 million invested, up to 30% for $300 million.

What the port said in 2026

June 25, 2026 board minutes: the port is "not incentivizing or actively recruiting any data centers. Where local communities have felt those data center projects are appropriate, they may be located in UIPA project areas." The port says it follows Gov. Cox's Executive Order 2026-03 on data center deals. At an Oct. 21, 2026 legislative hearing, the port's director said the West Weber sewer plant "potentially" could be used by a data center. So Weber County's zoning decides.

2026 laws that changed the rules

H.B. 507: new rules for deals with data centers and other big power users, a state savings account, and more records kept private. S.B. 225: water rules now follow local standards "rather than fixed statewide thresholds," and there's more "flexibility" in how the port gives tax money back to businesses. S.B. 206: tax changes. (These are the port's own summaries.)

If a data center came, would it pay its share?
A data center would pay full property tax like anyone else. But for 25 years, 75% of its tax growth would go to the port, not to schools, the county, the fire district or the park. Under the 2024 plan, a big new business could also get 10% to 30% of its tax growth back. The port now says it isn't offering data centers deals, and H.B. 507 sets new rules. The sewer it could use was paid for in part with federal relief money and a port loan.

Not checked yet: whether county zoning lets a data center go in the business park or the rest of the port area, and whether anyone has proposed one. The law summaries are the port's; the actual bills are linked in the sources.

For the public as a whole

Does this help, hurt, or neither?

Helps
  • The business park's roads, water and sewer are paid back by the builder's own land through the PID, not by taxpayers in general.
  • Land for parks: BlackPine gave about 35 acres for a park. Bennet must keep 1 acre of open space for every 34 homes, plus a park and trail along the Weber River.
  • While the $7,500 went to the park districts, it let them build parks without raising taxes.
  • The port sets aside some of its money for wetlands. After 25 years, all the tax goes back to local budgets.
Hurts
  • The parks got land but no money to keep it up. For 25 years, 75% of the business park's tax growth goes to the port. The county heard about this money problem in March 2023 and still moved BlackPine into the port that fall. On Nov. 2, the suggested fix was for park districts to raise taxes on residents.
  • The $7,500 has no clear public rule: no county law, no public total, and different explanations in 2024 and 2026. Opening it up to roads was talked about without a public hearing.
  • The builder's partners run the PID board, and that board approved the tax-sharing deals with the port. They disclosed it, but the same people are on both sides of the deal.
  • Leftover federal relief money can pass through the county to that builder-run PID.
  • The land went from farm zoning to up to 6 homes an acre. Froerer said on the record that buyers carry the $7,500.
  • The area sits next to Great Salt Lake wetlands, and the Weber River buffer was changed in December 2024.
Done right, or neither

Meetings were announced, minutes are posted, the PID board members filed sworn forms, the PID posts its records, and the bond notice was published.

On balance it leans toward hurting on following the money. On openness it's mixed: the papers are posted, but the explanations changed.

Still open

What an auditor would still check, most important first

  1. 1 · Follow the $7,500
    How much each park district got, from which projects, whether any is held in escrow, and what it was spent on. The park districts' yearly reports on Transparent Utah and with the State Auditor should show some of this.
  2. 2 · The deals that pay the builder back
    The Oct. 3, 2024 PID tax-sharing deals and the port's May 20, 2024 contract with PCC Land. Does the port's tax money pay BlackPine back for its own roads and sewer? The bond sale papers should explain it.
  3. 3 · Federal and port money for the sewer
    How much federal relief money and port loan money actually went to the PID, and whether the bonds also paid for the same pump station. That takes matching the payments to the same bills.
  4. 4 · Bennet's future buyers
    Is Bennet's land headed into a PID, the port area, or both? Its contract gives up the right to protest "on behalf of… all future owners." If PID charges land on houses, buyers pay them for up to 30 years.
  5. 5 · The park deed
    Was the 35-acre park actually deeded to West Warren, and when? The county recorder would show it.
  6. 6 · Campaign money
    Did Stephens, Hansen, the Becks or Curriden give to Harvey, Froerer or Bolos? State campaign reports would show it.

Also open: who Brad Margetts and Sam Hartman are and who owns GBP PCC, LLC; the Nov. 9, 2026 work session video on the Bennet addition and impact fees; the county's own copy of the Sept. 12, 2023 port resolution; the Aug. 12, 2025 Bennet vote count; and the county code Froerer called "ordinance." Whether requiring a "voluntary" gift before taking applications is legal is a question for lawyers or the state; this page reports what the records say.
