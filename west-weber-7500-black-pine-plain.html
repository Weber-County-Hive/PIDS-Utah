$7,500 a Home, in Plain Words

A short, simple version of the full case file.

Posted Nov. 9, 2026 · Updated Nov. 9, 2026
Want all the details and sources? Read the full case file

What is happening?

Farmland west of Ogden is being turned into houses. When the county rezones farmland for more homes, builders have agreed to give $7,500 for each new home for parks.

In 2024, the county said that money goes to the local park districts, and the park districts decide how to spend it. On Nov. 2, 2026, county staff said it's the county's money instead. Commissioner Gage Froerer said he'd like it to pay for roads too. No vote was taken.

What did the county say before?

In June 2024 the commission chair said the $7,500 was "a decision by the park district legislative body." A county contract that year said the park district has "full discretion" over the money. Froerer voted for these deals.

The park and the port

In 2023, a developer called BlackPine gave land for a park to the West Warren park district. A few months later, the county put BlackPine's business park into an Inland Port area. That's a state program.

Businesses in the port area pay full property tax
↓
For 25 years: 75% of the new growth goes to the port for roads and sewer
↓
Only 25% goes to schools, the county, the fire district and the park

So the park got land, but not the tax money to take care of it.

Who doesn't get the tax growth?

Our estimate, using the port's own plan, over 25 years:

Weber County School District$206.5 million
Weber County$71.3 million
Weber Fire District$43.6 million
West Warren park$5.2–5.8 million

All local budgets together: about $360.8 million. Federal relief money and a port loan, $5 million each, also help pay for the business park's sewer.

Who runs the money?

The business park has a PID, a special district that borrows money for roads and sewer. Two of BlackPine's own partners run its board. They wrote that down on sworn forms. That board approved deals sharing tax money with the port.

Who pays for the growth?

  • Business tax growth mostly goes to the port, not the park.
  • Froerer said park districts can "go to the public and raise taxes." That would fall on people who already live there.
  • Froerer also said the $7,500 "goes on the backs of the prospective homeowner."
  • Meanwhile, the farm being readied for up to 3,300 homes is still taxed as farmland.

Is $7,500 an impact fee?

No. An impact fee needs a state-required study, a public hearing, and one rule for everyone, and it generally must be spent within six years or given back. The $7,500 is written into each builder's contract one at a time and called a "voluntary" gift.

What about data centers?

The port's 2024 plan listed data centers among the businesses it favored. In 2026 the port said it isn't giving data centers deals, but they "may be located" in port areas if the county allows it. A data center would pay full tax, but most of its tax growth would go to the port for 25 years.

Did the public get a vote?

No. None of this went on a ballot. Commissioners and appointed boards decided. The hearing that created the business park's PID lasted three minutes, and no one spoke.

The Hive's grade: D+ (for now)

Transparency D+, following the money D, environment C−. The grade can change as more records come in.

What can I ask?

Ask the Weber County Commission: Who should pay for growth? Will any change to the $7,500 get a public hearing? Which roads would it pay for? Ask your park district how much $7,500 money it has received and what it built.
